   - AI systems can disproportionately affect vulnerable groups, necessitating additional protections. This category underscores the ethical considerations surrounding the deployment of AI systems in ways that could lead to discrimination or exploitation.

   - These risks refer to potential widespread negative impacts that could arise from using AI technologies, especially general-purpose AI models. Systemic risks emphasize the necessity for thorough risk assessments to mitigate adverse effects on society or the economy.

   - Various obligations are defined under the Regulation that AI systems must fulfill, particularly regarding safety standards, transparency, and ethical implications. Compliance frameworks ensure that applicable AI systems are evaluated for risks and regulated accordingly.

### Summary
The differentiation of AI systems into these risk categories is vital for regulatory compliance and for safeguarding individual rights and societal norms, especially in contexts where AI technologies are deployed in sensitive areas.

### Prohibited AI Systems and Practices under the AI Act

The AI Act outlines specific prohibitions aimed at safeguarding individuals' rights and ensuring ethical standards in the deployment of AI technologies. Below are the primary categories of AI systems and practices that are explicitly prohibited:

1. **Subliminal Techniques**:
   - The Act prohibits the use of AI systems that deploy subliminal techniques beyond a person's conscious awareness or use manipulative or deceptive methods that significantly distort a person's ability to make informed decisions.

2. **Exploitation of Vulnerabilities**:
   - AI systems that exploit the vulnerabilities of individuals or specific groups based on factors such as age, disability, or socio-economic status are banned. This prohibition is aimed at preventing any form of abuse that may lead to significant harm.

3. **Social Scoring**:
   - The use of AI systems for the evaluation or classification of natural persons based on social behavior, known characteristics, or inferred personal attributes is prohibited. This can lead to unjust treatment of individuals based on social scores.

4. **Criminal Profiling**:
   - AI systems that assess or predict the risk of a natural person committing a criminal offense based solely on profiling or personality traits are prohibited. However, systems supporting human assessments based on objective facts are exempt from this prohibition.

5. **Facial Recognition and Emotion Detection**:
   - AI systems that create or expand databases for facial recognition without proper oversight or that aim to detect emotions in specific contexts such as workplaces or educational institutions are prohibited unless they serve medical or safety purposes.

### Conclusion

These prohibitions are part of a broader regulatory framework designed to ensure that AI systems uphold fundamental rights, prevent discrimination, and foster ethical practices in their development and application.

### User Notification When Interacting with AI Systems

When users are interacting with AI systems, particularly those designed to interact directly with natural persons or to handle sensitive information, there are specific obligations designed to ensure transparency. Here are the key points regarding user notifications:

1. **Clear Notification Requirement**: Users must be clearly informed when they are interacting with an AI system. This requirement applies unless the interaction is evident based on reasonable discernment by the user. If users are engaging with AI systems that process biometric data to identify or infer emotions, they should also receive specific notifications.

2. **Vulnerable Groups Consideration**: Special attention must be given when the AI system interacts with vulnerable groups, such as those with disabilities or older individuals. Notifications provided to these groups should be accessible and comprehensible, ensuring that their rights are protected effectively.

3. **Transparency in AI Outputs**: For AI systems that generate or manipulate content (e.g., deep fakes), providers must disclose that the content produced has been artificially generated or manipulated. This transparency obligation helps maintain accountability and enriches user understanding of the content they are engaging with.

4. **Timing of Notifications**: The information about the nature of the interaction should be provided distinctly at the latest by the time of the first interaction, ensuring that users are aware of their engagement with AI technologies right from the beginning.

5. **Compliance with Existing Laws**: The notifications and interactions with AI systems must also comply with relevant legal frameworks, such as the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and other national regulations that enforce personal data protection and privacy rights.

By following these guidelines, the interaction with AI systems can be more ethical and transparent, thereby enhancing trust and understanding among users.

### AI Systems with Minimal Risks

AI systems classified as having minimal risks are typically those that perform limited, narrow, procedural tasks and do not materially influence decision-making outcomes in significant ways. Such systems are not likely to pose a substantial threat to health, safety, or the fundamental rights of individuals. The determination of minimal risk is based on specific conditions outlined in relevant regulations.

#### Conditions for Classification as Minimal Risk

1. **Narrow Procedural Tasks**: AI systems that are designed to carry out straightforward tasks, such as data classification or document processing, may be categorized as minimal risk. Their functions are usually non-intrusive and lack significant impact on users' lives.

2. **Supporting Human Activities**: Systems intended to enhance or improve previously completed human activities without replacing or impacting the original assessment are also viewed as minimal risk. For instance, they could aid in refining language or organizing information.

3. **Detection of Patterns**: AI systems used solely for identifying patterns or reporting deviations from expected outcomes, while ensuring that any conclusions drawn are based on prior human assessments, may qualify for minimal risk due to their limited scope.

4. **Preparatory Tasks**: AI systems that perform preparatory functions before an assessment—without influencing the actual decision—are classified as minimal risk, as their direct impact on outcomes is considerably reduced.

5. **Non-influential Systems**: Certain AI systems may not substantially affect the outcome of decision-making processes. For example, those used for administrative tasks where decisions are primarily human-driven and any AI contribution does not alter the core outcomes can fall under this category.

These classifications reflect a regulatory understanding aimed at ensuring a balanced approach to AI development, particularly focusing on transparency and safety.

   - Administrative fines collected contribute to the budget of the European Union.

### Differences Between Deployer and Provider

**Deployer:**
- A deployer is an individual or organization that implements and operates artificial intelligence (AI) systems, particularly those classified as high-risk. They are responsible for putting AI systems into service and supervising their performance while ensuring compliance with regulations.
- Deployers must possess a thorough understanding of how AI systems work to interpret their outputs accurately. They are also engaged in tasks such as managing operational standards and ensuring that users are trained to utilize the systems effectively.
- They must follow the instructions for use associated with the AI systems and manage any risks that arise from their deployment. Deployers have specific obligations defined by regulations related to high-risk AI systems, including monitoring the functionality of these systems, maintaining logs, and informing relevant authorities about incidents.

**Provider:**
- A provider refers to a natural or legal person, including public authorities and agencies, that develops or develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model, places it on the market, or puts it into service under their own name or trademark.
- Providers are responsible for the creation, testing, documentation, and the declaration of conformity for the AI systems to ensure they meet established safety and regulatory standards. They must design the systems with specific intended purposes in mind and communicate these to deployers.
- Providers also play a critical role in ensuring that the AI systems comply with the necessary regulations before deployment. They are tasked with developing risk management measures and providing necessary support and documentation to deployers.

### Conclusion
The primary distinction between deployers and providers lies in their roles: deployers implement and manage AI systems within operational contexts, while providers are involved in the development and compliance aspects of these AI systems. Deployers adapt the systems for use, whereas providers design and ensure the systems meet legal standards.
